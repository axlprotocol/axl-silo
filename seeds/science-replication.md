SITUATION:
A high-profile paper in Nature Neuroscience claims that a novel peptide (NX-7) reverses age-related cognitive decline in mice with effect size d=1.8. The paper has 847 citations in 14 months. Three independent labs have attempted replication.

REPLICATION ATTEMPTS:
- Lab A (MIT): n=60 mice. Effect size d=0.3, p=0.12. Did NOT replicate.
- Lab B (Max Planck): n=45 mice. Effect size d=0.4, p=0.08. Did NOT replicate.
- Lab C (Tokyo): n=80 mice. Effect size d=1.6, p<0.001. REPLICATED (but used different strain of mice).

ORIGINAL PAPER CONCERNS:
- Sample size: n=12 per group (very small for the claimed effect)
- No pre-registration
- 6 statistical tests reported, no multiple comparison correction
- First author is a PhD student; PI has 3 previous corrections
- Raw data not shared despite journal policy
- Funding from a pharmaceutical company developing NX-7 analogs

NAMED ENTITIES:
1. @Statistician — Biostatistics expert. Focused on methodology, power analysis, p-values.
2. @Replicator_A — MIT lab lead. Failed to replicate. Concerned about sample size.
3. @Replicator_C — Tokyo lab lead. Claims successful replication. Different mouse strain.
4. @Editor — Journal editor. Must decide on retraction, correction, or expression of concern.
5. @Ethics_Officer — Research integrity. Investigating undisclosed conflicts of interest.
6. @Original_Author — Defends the paper. Claims strain differences explain failed replications.

THE QUESTION:
Given the replication attempts, methodological concerns, and conflicts of interest:
Should the journal retract the paper, issue a correction, publish an expression of concern, or take no action?
